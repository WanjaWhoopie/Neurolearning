IStation one · Admission desk

Why EEG is where neuro AI starts

In 1924 the psychiatrist Hans Berger put electrodes on his son's scalp and recorded the first human electroencephalogram. He saw a stately wave, about ten per second, that appeared when the eyes closed and vanished when they opened. He named it alpha. A century later the same measurement, voltage between points on the scalp, is still the only brain signal that is non-invasive, millisecond-fast, portable and cheap all at once.

For you, coming from machine learning: EEG is a multivariate time series. 8 to 256 channels, sampled 250 to 1000 times a second, with amplitudes of tens of microvolts, about a millionth of an AA battery. It runs epilepsy monitoring, sleep staging, anaesthesia depth and brain–computer interfaces today, and it is the data behind most public brain-decoding benchmarks.

N.B.

EEG trades space for time. It knows when to the millisecond and where only to the centimetre. Everything in this laboratory follows from that trade.

IIStation two · The source

What is actually making the voltage

The usual guess, that EEG records neurons firing, is wrong in a way that matters. A spike lasts a millisecond and neighbours fire out of step, so their fields cancel long before the scalp. What EEG sees is slower.

  • Postsynaptic potentials. When a synapse releases transmitter onto a dendrite, the membrane voltage shifts for 10–100 ms. Slow enough to overlap and add.
  • Pyramidal neurons. Large cortical cells whose long apical dendrites all point the same way, at right angles to the surface, like trees in a plantation.
  • Dipoles. An excitatory synapse near the top pulls positive ions in there (a sink) and current leaves near the cell body (a source). The cell becomes a tiny battery.

One battery is hopeless. But the dendrites are parallel, so the batteries add. When some six square centimetres of cortex receive input at the same moment, the sum is finally large enough to measure through bone. EEG therefore measures synchrony, not effort. A region working hard out of step is invisible. A region idling in lockstep is loud.

N.B.

EEG is the summed postsynaptic potentials of synchronised, parallel pyramidal neurons. Not spikes.

IIIStation three · Section through the head

Through the skull, and why EEG is blurry

Fig. 3 · a dipole under scalp, skull and fluiddrag the arrow along the cortex

Between the cortex and the electrode lie cerebrospinal fluid, skull and scalp. Current has to pass through all three. This is volume conduction, and the skull is a wretched conductor, dozens of times more resistive than the tissue around it.

  • Attenuation. Millivolts at the cortex arrive as tens of microvolts.
  • Smearing. The skull low-pass filters across space. A focal source spreads over many electrodes, and every electrode hears a weighted mix of many sources. Toggle the skull off in the figure and watch the pattern sharpen.
  • Orientation. Drag the dipole onto the crown of a gyrus: it points straight at the scalp and reads strongly. Drag it down the wall of the sulcus: it lies sideways, the scalp map weakens and flips sign across the fold. Those tangential sources are the ones MEG prefers.

Going backwards, from scalp map to source, is the inverse problem. It is ill-posed: infinitely many source arrangements give the same map. Source localisation (dipole fitting, beamformers, sLORETA) only works by adding assumptions.

N.B.

Every channel is a mixture. Hold on to that sentence; it is why ICA works at station VII.

An EEG channel is a difference: amplifier output = active electrode − reference electrode, with a third, the ground, to reject noise common to both. People trip over the consequence for years: there is no such thing as the voltage at an electrode. Every waveform depends on the reference you chose, whether linked mastoids, Cz, or the average of all channels. Re-referencing is a linear operation you can redo offline, and it changes every channel.

Electrodes are silver/silver-chloride with gel; dry ones trade signal for convenience. Before recording, the technician checks impedance, ideally under 10 kΩ. The amplifier then digitises at 250–1000 Hz, and by Nyquist you can only represent frequencies below half that rate. Peel the skin away above and look at what each site sits over: C3 on the hand area of motor cortex, O1 on visual cortex, Fp1 just above the eye.

VStation five · The rhythm cabinet

Five bands everyone speaks in

Raw EEG looks like noise until you learn its vocabulary. Populations of neurons, paced by loops between thalamus and cortex and by local inhibitory circuits, oscillate. The frequency tracks the state of the brain so reliably that the field named the bands with Greek letters a century ago and never stopped. The borders are convention. The correlations are real.

Turn the dial through the states. The pen shows the time series; the analyser beside it shows the same thing the way your model will probably see it, as power per frequency.

Two facts that matter for ML

The mu rhythm, an alpha-range wave over motor cortex, drops when you move a hand or merely imagine moving it, and it drops on the opposite side of the head. That drop, event-related desynchronisation, is the signal behind motor-imagery BCIs.

Scalp gamma shares its frequencies with scalp muscle. Dramatic gamma results from scalp EEG deserve suspicion, which brings us to the next ward.

VIStation six · The artifact ward

Most of your signal is not brain

Fig. 6 · one channel, contaminated to orderstudy mode
streak 0 · best 0
Switch the contaminants on one at a time and learn each signature. Then take the drill: the ward injects one at random and you name it.

Brain EEG is tens of microvolts. A blink is hundreds. A clenched jaw can be thousands. Before you model anything you need an eye for the impostors. Each has a signature in where it appears, what shape it takes, and what frequency it lives at.

  • Blink. A huge slow hill, biggest at Fp1 and Fp2. The eyeball is itself a dipole and the lid sweeps across it.
  • Eye movement. A step, opposite in sign at F7 and F8.
  • Muscle. Spiky broadband fuzz above 20 Hz from jaw, forehead or neck. The gamma impostor.
  • Mains. A relentless pure 50 Hz (60 in the Americas).
  • Drift and pop. A wandering baseline from sweat or drying gel; a sudden jump when a contact slips.
  • Heartbeat. A spike about once a second. Pen 4 on the masthead recorder shows it.
N.B.

If a classifier scores suspiciously well, check whether it learned blinks, jaw tension or mains differences between conditions. This is the commonest failure in published EEG machine learning.

VIIStation seven · The cleaning bench

Filters, and the unmixing machine

Every project passes down the same bench, and this is what MNE-Python is for: inspect the raw data, filter, re-reference, mark and interpolate bad channels, run ICA, cut into epochs around events, reject what is still bad, baseline-correct.

Filtering removes what cannot be brain: a high-pass at 0.1–1 Hz for drift, a notch at 50 or 60 for mains, a low-pass at 40–100 for fuzz. Filters have teeth, though. They smear energy in time, and an aggressive high-pass can distort slow ERP components or invent effects that were never there. Keep the high-pass at or below about 0.3 Hz for ERP work, filter the continuous recording rather than short epochs, and report your settings.

Independent component analysis is the field's favourite trick. Because the skull mixes sources linearly, ICA can unmix the channels into statistically independent components. Blinks and mains come out as their own components. You delete them and mix the rest back.

Technician's rule: judge a component by its map and its spectrum together. Frontal and slow is an eye. Everywhere and exactly 50 Hz is the wall socket. Posterior with a 10 Hz bump is the brain you came for.

VIIIStation eight · The averager

How raw voltage becomes model input

Fig. 8 · averaging computer, channel Pzthin pen: newest trial · heavy pen: running average
N = 000
A response of 8 µV is hidden in every trial under background EEG several times its size. One trial shows nothing. Keep adding.

Event-related potentials

Present a stimulus many times, align the epochs, average. Background EEG is unrelated to the stimulus and averages toward zero. The brain's time-locked response survives. Signal-to-noise grows as √N, so a hundred trials buys a tenfold cleaner wave.

The classics: P300, a parietal positive wave 300–500 ms after a rare, attended stimulus, the engine of P300 spellers. N170 for faces. ERN, the brain noticing its own mistake within 100 ms. SSVEP: stare at a light flickering at 12 Hz and visual cortex hums at exactly 12 Hz.

Spectral and spatial features

  • Band power by Welch's method. The workhorse of sleep staging.
  • The 1/f slope. EEG spectra fall as 1/f. specparam separates true peaks from that background, and the slope itself tracks excitation and inhibition.
  • Time–frequency maps by Morlet wavelets. An image a CNN can eat.
  • Common spatial patterns. Channel weightings whose variance differs most between two classes.
  • Covariance on the Riemannian manifold. Treat each trial's channel covariance as a point on a curved space and classify there. Unreasonably effective.
  • Connectivity. Coherence and phase locking between channels, easily faked by one source leaking into two electrodes.
IXStation nine · The decoding office

Machine learning on EEG, honestly

Recipes that win

  • Motor imagery: band-pass 8–30 Hz, CSP, LDA. Decades old and still a brutal baseline.
  • P300: xDAWN or downsampled epochs into regularised LDA, or a Riemannian classifier.
  • SSVEP: canonical correlation against the flicker frequencies.
  • Deep learning: EEGNet and ShallowConvNet, small CNNs whose first layers are a learned band-pass and a learned spatial filter. On most tasks they match the classical pipelines rather than crush them. Self-supervised foundation models pretrained on large unlabelled corpora (BENDR, LaBraM and their successors) are promising and not yet a settled story.

Why your ImageNet instincts fail here

  • Signal-to-noise. The label-relevant part is a few percent of the variance.
  • Non-stationarity. Gel dries, attention wanders. A model can die between morning and afternoon.
  • People differ. Skull thickness and cortical folding vary, so cross-subject transfer is the central open problem.
  • Small data. A large academic set is a few dozen subjects.
  • Leakage. Trials seconds apart are correlated. Try the three splits on the gauge.

Choose how to divide train from test. The model and the recordings do not change. Only the honesty of the split does.

On the desk · two recent papers, and why to read them

NeurIPS 2025 · main track

REVE: A Foundation Model for EEG, Adapting to Any Setup with Large-Scale Pretraining on 25,000 Subjects

A masked-autoencoder model pretrained on more than 60,000 hours of EEG from 92 datasets. Its 4D positional encoding lets it take recordings of any length and any electrode layout. The authors report state-of-the-art results on ten downstream tasks, among them motor imagery, seizure detection and sleep staging, and release code, weights and tutorials.

Why read it: station IV showed that every dataset uses a different cap. This is the current answer to how one model can swallow all of them, and it is the model to try in work order 11-D.

arXiv 2510.21585project page
arXiv preprint · 2026 · Liu et al.

EEG-FM-Compass: Progress, Benchmarking, and Future Directions for EEG Foundation Models

A review of 55 EEG foundation models, then a benchmark of 12 open-source ones against specialist baselines on 13 datasets and nine BCI paradigms. Its abstract reports three findings: a frozen model with a linear probe is frequently not enough, specialist models trained from scratch stay competitive on many tasks, and larger models do not necessarily generalise better.

Why read it: it is the sceptical companion to REVE, and station IX's warning in published form. Read the two together before you believe any leaderboard.
